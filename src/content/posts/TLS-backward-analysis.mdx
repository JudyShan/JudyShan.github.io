Total least squares (TLS) is often regarded as a natural extension of least squares (LS).
While the latter has made fundamental influence in many areas thanks to its abundant theory and well-deveolped algorithms, the former is still attractive (but harder to solve) since it is more comprehensive with respect to considering all the errors in the data.
For a single right-hand side system $[A,b]$<Sidenote number={1}>my papers deal with the general multidimensional case $[A,B]$ and consider weighting between two perturbations.</Sidenote>, where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}(m \gg n)$ and $b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$, 
two problems are written as<Sidenote number={2}> Typically, we write LS as $\min_{x} \|Ax-b\|_{2}$. Here, we change to an equivalent form for comparison.</Sidenote>
$$
\min_{\Delta A,\Delta b}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta A, \Delta b
\end{bmatrix}
\right\|_{\mathrm F}
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
(A+\Delta A)x=b+\Delta b,
\tag{TLS}
$$

$$
\min_{\Delta b}
\left\|\Delta b\right\|_2
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
Ax=b+\Delta b.
\tag{LS}
$$
This viewpoint of 'natural extension' is compelling at the level of data modeling, and it also helps explain why statisticians were among the first to study them.
Yet the analogy could be much less convincing as we will see.
Despite a substantial body of theoretical work, we still lack TLS algorithms that simultaneously satisfy the practical requirements one would expect of a satisfactory numerical solver <Sidenote number={3} label="Explanation of a satisfactory numerical solver">By “satisfactory,” I mean methods that are fast in large-scale problems, remain reasonably accurate for at least moderately ill-conditioned problems, and are numerically stable.</Sidenote>.

> A big motivation is: If we could solve a TLS as fast and well as solving an LS under the same data set, we should not hesitate to choose TLS in general.

In my view, things are partly obscured by the way the subject has developed, and TLS indeed deserves more emphasis than it has received —— not simply through further abstraction, but through a reconsideration of which structures are actually useful for computation.
One such perspective concerns backward error (see Section 2). 

In this post, I will outline some key ideas developed in my recent papers on
[the connections and differences between LS and TLS](/publications/shan2025optimal/)
and
[efficient backward error estimation for multidimensional TLS](/publications/shan2026EfficientBE/),
and offer a few reflections that may be useful for future work.
## 1. From LS to TLS: are they essentially related and how?
By relating to low-rank approximation, or [Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-rank_approximation), one could understand that $[x_{\mathrm{TLS}};-1]$ is parallel to the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of $[A,b]$.
I will not repeat [basic results of TLS](https://doi.org/10.1137/0717073) here.
Consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of this augmented matrix
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A, b
\end{bmatrix}
=
U\Sigma V^\top,
\qquad
V=
\begin{bmatrix}
V_{11} & v_{12}\\
v_{21}^\top & v_{22}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $v_{22}$ is a scalar.
To guarantee the existence and uniqueness of a TLS solution, we assume the _genericity condition_ throughout this post

$$
\sigma_n\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
A, b
\end{bmatrix}
\right)
>
\sigma_{n+1}\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
A, b
\end{bmatrix}
\right),
\qquad
v_{22}\neq 0.
$$

People derive, based on the above knowledge, a normal equation for TLS as $\bf \left(A^\top A-\sigma_{n+1}^{2}([A,b])I\right)x=A^\top b$, which I will call the _classical normal equation_.
When it comes to LS, the normal equation is much more elegant as $\bf A^\top r=0$ where $r=b-Ax$ is the residual.
If we want to potentially leverage LS thoery, we definitely need new results that can treat TLS as a modified LS.
The following theorem achieves the goal by giving a _structural normal equation_, and more importantly, show the intrinsic quantities of TLS.

<Statement
  type="theorem"
  number="1.1"
  title="Necessary and sufficient conditions for the TLS solution"
>

Suppose that the genericity condition above holds. Then
$x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the true solution to the problem (TLS) if and only if the following two conditions hold:

$$
\left(A+bx^\top\right)^\top(Ax-b)=0,
\tag{2.1a}
$$

and

$$
\min_{\substack{
  0\neq y\in
  \mathcal{R}\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x;-1
    \end{bmatrix}
  \right)
}}
\frac{
  \left\|
    \begin{bmatrix}
      A, b
    \end{bmatrix}y
  \right\|
}{
  \lVert y\rVert
}
<
\min_{\substack{
  0\neq y\in
  \mathcal{R}\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
      I_n;
      x^\top
    \end{bmatrix}
  \right)
}}
\frac{
  \left\|
    \begin{bmatrix}
      A, b
    \end{bmatrix}y
  \right\|
}{
  \lVert y\rVert
}.
\tag{2.1b}
$$
Here, $\mathcal{R}(M)$ denotes the range of $M$.

</Statement>
I shall present only the key parts of the proof which shed a light on the TLS structure. Extension to multidimensional cases is straightforward.
<Statement type="proof">

Let the SVD of $\begin{bmatrix}A, b\end{bmatrix}$, with the singular values arranged in nonincreasing order, be
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A, b
\end{bmatrix}
=
\left(
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
u_i\sigma_i v_i^\top
\right)
+
u_{n+1}\sigma_{n+1}v_{n+1}^\top
\coloneqq
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat A, \widehat b
\end{bmatrix}
+
u_{n+1}\sigma_{n+1}v_{n+1}^\top.
$$

Under the genericity condition, $x$ is the true solution of the TLS problem
if and only if $\widehat A x-\widehat b=0$.

Define

$$
T_1
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_n\\
x^\top
\end{bmatrix}
\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}, \quad
T_2
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}\left(I+x^\top x\right)^{-1/2}.
$$

It is straightforward to verify that $T_1^\top T_1=I_n, T_2^\top T_2=1, T_1^\top T_2=0$, and $T_1T_1^\top+T_2T_2^\top=I_{n+1}$.

Consequently, $\begin{bmatrix}T_1, T_2\end{bmatrix}$ is an orthogonal matrix. Now define
$$
\widetilde A=\left(A+bx^\top\right)\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}\coloneqq
\begin{bmatrix}
A, b
\end{bmatrix}T_1,
\quad 
\widetilde r=\frac{Ax-b}{\sqrt{1+x^\top x}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}A, b
\end{bmatrix}T_2 .
$$

The first condition in the theorem is equivalent to<Sidenote number={4}>It can be further reduced to (2.1a) by the invertibility of two auxiliary matrices (scalar).</Sidenote>

$$
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r=0,
$$

whereas the second condition is equivalent to<Sidenote number={5}> It will be helpful to think of $\|\widetilde r\|_2$ as $\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}(r)$.</Sidenote>

$$
\left\|\widetilde r\right\|_2
<
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\widetilde A\right).
$$

Therefore, it remains to prove the equivalent characterization

$$
\boxed{
x\text{ is the true TLS solution}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\left\|\widetilde r\right\|_2
<
\sigma_{\min}\left(\widetilde A\right)
}.
$$

</Statement>
It can be proven that there always exists a neighbourhood of $x_{\mathrm{TLS}}$ such that (2.1b) uniformly holds.
This is a good news for iterative ideas. 
One imprtant takeaway here is the _structural normal equation_, not exactly (2.1a), but 
$$
\widetilde A^\top \widetilde r=0, \quad \mathrm{where} \quad \widetilde{A}=\left(A+bx^\top\right)\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}, \widetilde{r}=\frac{Ax-b}{\sqrt{1+\|x\|^{2}}},
$$
which tells that TLS cares about a complicated coupling of $A,b$ and the solution $x$, rather than just making the residual orthogonal to a fixed plane.
Such differences paralyze some famous techniques that play a significant role in making LS trustworthy, such as iterative refinement.
But it is still worthy to ask under what conditions an LS solution will not be far away from the according TLS solution.
I provide a perspective as follows.

<Statement
  type="theorem"
  number="1.2"
  title="Component-wise error of a backward stable LS solution to the true TLS solution"
>

Given a backward stable LS solution $\hat{x}$ to an overdetermined system $[A,b]$, the component-wise error of $x_{\mathrm{TLS}}$ to $\hat{x}$ with respect to the right singular vectors of $A$, is upper-bounded for $i=1,2,\ldots,n$ as

$$
\left|v_i^{\top}\left(\left(1-\frac{{\sigma_{n+1}([A,b])}^{2}}{\sigma_i(A)^{2}}\right)x_{\mathrm{TLS}}-\hat{x}\right)\right|
\lesssim
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}
\|\hat{x}\|\|[A,b]\|\,u+\frac{1}{\sigma_i^{2}}\|b-A\hat{x}\|\|[A,b]\|\,u,
$$
where $u$ is the unit roundoff. The notation $\lesssim$ means that the left-hand side is bounded above by $C(m,n)$ times the right-hand side for some low-degree polynomial $C(m,n)$.
</Statement>
Backward stability will be explained in details later. For readers who are not familiar, just think of a backward stable solution as a best candidate that we can ever expect from a numerical method.

From the above, LS information can be exploited under certain conditions.
Also, normal condition number, defined by the ratio between the maximum and minimum singular values of a matrix, fails to characterize the difficulty of a TLS system but spectral gap (or [effective condition number](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01400115)) does.
Specificlly, a small LS residual indicates a similar TLS solution, which shows that a TLS modeling is not very necessary for a relatively consistent system.

<Statement
  type="proposition"
  number="1.3"
  title="The residual of the LS solution matters"
>

Let $x_{\mathrm{LS}}$ be the LS solution and $r_{\mathrm{LS}}=b-Ax_{\mathrm{LS}}$ be its residual. 
Denote $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}(A)/\sigma_{\min}(A)$ as the normal condition number of $A$ and follow the previous notation. 
If there is a gap between $1$ and $\kappa\|r_{\mathrm{LS}}\|<1$, then the ratio $\sigma_{n+1}([A,b])/\sigma_n(A)$ is away from $1$.

</Statement>

There are other gaps between these two problems.

## 2. Backward error and its efficient computation

Numerical algorithms run in finite precision, so even a mathematically correct method rarely returns
the exact solution of the data supplied to it. A solver is called _backward stable_ if its computed
output is the exact solution of a nearby problem, with the required change in the input comparable
to the rounding errors introduced during the computation. This point of view deliberately separates
the behavior of the algorithm from the conditioning of the problem: a backward-stable answer can
still have a large forward error when the underlying problem is ill-conditioned.

Backward stability is one reason to study the backward error of TLS. Before we can prove that a TLS
solver is stable, or decide when an iterative method should stop, we need a quantitative answer to
the following question:

> How much must the original data be perturbed before $\widetilde x$ becomes an exact TLS solution?

The question is especially natural for TLS because both $A$ and $b$ are already assumed to contain
errors. A small backward error certifies that the computed answer solves a nearby problem of the
same type, even when comparison with the unknown exact solution is impossible. For stability
analysis we want an exact characterization; for actual solvers we also need a reliable estimate
that is inexpensive enough to evaluate repeatedly. In large-scale problems, this second requirement
leads naturally to randomized algorithms.

### 2.1 An optimal backward error

<Statement type="definition" number="2.1" title="TLS backward error">

For a computed approximation $\widetilde x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, define

$$
\tau(\widetilde x)
=
\min_{E,f}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}
E,\ f
\end{bmatrix}
\right\|_{\mathrm F}
$$

subject to the structural normal equation for the perturbed data,

$$
\left((A+E)+(b+f)\widetilde x^\top\right)^\top
\left((A+E)\widetilde x-(b+f)\right)=0.
$$

</Statement>

The normalized quantity $\tau(\widetilde x)/\|[A,b]\|_{\mathrm F}$ measures the size of the smallest
relative change to the data. In the usual normwise sense, a TLS algorithm is backward stable when
this ratio is bounded by a modest dimension-dependent constant times the unit roundoff $u$.

Strictly speaking, the second, spectral-separation condition in Theorem 1.1 must also hold before
$\widetilde x$ is the global TLS solution of the nearby problem. The definition above uses the
relaxed feasible set determined by the structural normal equation. This relaxation is useful rather
than merely convenient: as $\widetilde x$ approaches the true TLS solution, the optimal perturbation
obtained from this set also satisfies the missing separation condition. Thus the relaxed backward
error is asymptotically the genuine one near the solution.

The apparently nonlinear minimization above has a concise closed form. Define the two intrinsic
quantities

$$
\widetilde A
=
\left(A+b\widetilde x^\top\right)
\left(I_n+\widetilde x\widetilde x^\top\right)^{-1/2},
\qquad
\widetilde r
=
\frac{b-A\widetilde x}
{\sqrt{1+\|\widetilde x\|_2^2}}.
\tag{2.2}
$$

These are precisely the modified matrix and scaled residual that appeared in Section 1, up to the
harmless choice of sign for the residual.

<Statement type="theorem" number="2.2" title="Optimal backward-error formula">

Let

$$
\lambda
=
\lambda_{\min}\!\left(
\widetilde A\widetilde A^\top
-
\widetilde r\widetilde r^\top
\right).
$$

Then the optimal backward error is

$$
\boxed{
\tau(\widetilde x)
=
\left[
\|\widetilde r\|_2^2
+
\min\{0,\lambda\}
\right]^{1/2}
}.
\tag{2.3}
$$

</Statement>

Formula (2.3) separates two effects. The first term measures the scaled residual, while the
eigenvalue correction detects whether a coupled perturbation of $A$ and $b$ can make
$\widetilde x$ an exact TLS solution more cheaply than correcting the residual alone. This is why
$\|b-A\widetilde x\|_2$ by itself is not a reliable TLS certificate.

There is nevertheless a computational problem: evaluating $\lambda$ directly involves an
$m\times m$ eigenvalue problem. For a tall data matrix with $m\gg n$, the exact formula is therefore
more useful as a theoretical characterization than as an implementation.

### 2.2 A computable estimate

The large eigenvalue problem can be replaced by the following quantity involving only an
$n\times n$ positive-definite matrix:

$$
\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)
=
\left\|
\left(
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde A
+
\|\widetilde r\|_2^2 I_n
\right)^{-1/2}
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r
\right\|_2.
\tag{2.4}
$$

<Statement type="theorem" number="2.3" title="A tight computable estimate">

For every computed approximation $\widetilde x$,

$$
\boxed{
\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)
\leq
\tau(\widetilde x)
\leq
\sqrt{2}\,\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)
}.
\tag{2.5}
$$

</Statement>

Hence $\widetilde\tau$ is not a heuristic: it approximates the optimal backward error to
within a factor of $\sqrt{2}$. If
$\widetilde A=U\Sigma V^\top$, it can also be evaluated without explicitly forming a matrix
inverse square root:

$$
\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)^2
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\sigma_i^2}
{\sigma_i^2+\|\widetilde r\|_2^2}
\left(u_i^\top\widetilde r\right)^2.
\tag{2.6}
$$

This already avoids an $m\times m$ eigendecomposition, but forming a full factorization of
$\widetilde A$ may still be expensive when the number of observations is very large.
Randomized sketching gives one more reduction. Let $S\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times m}$ be a subspace
embedding for the columns of $[A,b]$ with distortion $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, and define

$$
\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x)
=
\left\|
\left(
(S\widetilde A)^\top(S\widetilde A)
+
\|\widetilde r\|_2^2 I_n
\right)^{-1/2}
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r
\right\|_2.
\tag{2.7}
$$

The sketch preserves a rigorous certificate:

$$
(1-\varepsilon)\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x)
\leq
\tau(\widetilde x)
\leq
\sqrt{2}(1+\varepsilon)
\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x).
\tag{2.8}
$$

With a sparse embedding, the resulting procedure has complexity $\mathcal{O}(mn\log n)$ and never
forms the large matrix in (2.3). The factor
$\left(I_n+\widetilde x\widetilde x^\top\right)^{-1/2}$ is only a rank-one update of the
identity, so $\widetilde A$ can also be formed efficiently. MATLAB implementations of the estimators
and the scripts used for the experiments below are available in the
[mTLS Efficient Backward Error Estimate code repository](https://github.com/JudyShan/mTLS-Efficient-Backward-Error-Estimate).

### 2.3 Are the bounds tight in practice?

The following experiment uses $m=3000$, $n=20$, and 200 random problems at each of three condition
numbers. The horizontal axis is the exact backward error divided by its estimate. The blue
distributions correspond to $\tau/\widetilde\tau$, which theory places between $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$;
the orange distributions use the sketched estimate. In the top row, which is the vector case studied
in this post, the ratios remain tightly concentrated even as $\kappa(A)$ increases from $10^8$ to
$10^{16}$. The bottom row shows the analogous behavior for ten right-hand sides.

### 2.4 Efficiency and use as a stopping criterion

The runtime grows approximately linearly with $n$ for fixed $m$. The left panel below is the vector
case; the remaining panels show that the same scaling persists for multiple right-hand sides. This
is the practical gain of estimating the backward error through a sketch rather than evaluating the
exact $m\times m$ eigenvalue formula.

An efficient certificate can also influence the design of the solver itself. In the next experiment,
the backward-error estimate is used to stop an iterative TLS method once it reaches the scale of
machine precision. The red stars mark the selected stopping iterations. Continuing afterward gives
essentially no improvement, so the certificate avoids unnecessary iterations while preserving the
attained accuracy.

<Figure
  path="/assets/img/tls-backward-error-stopping.png"

  caption="A backward-error stopping criterion terminates the iteration once further work no longer improves the computed solution."
/>

Finally, one should not replace the estimator by a superficially similar LS heuristic. The simple
quantity $\|\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r\|_2/\|\widetilde r\|_2$ can overestimate the true TLS
backward error by many orders of magnitude as the condition number grows, whereas the sketched
estimator remains close to the true value.

<Figure
  path="/assets/img/tls-backward-error-estimator-comparison.png"
  alt="Estimation-to-true-value ratio for the sketched estimator and a simple estimator over increasing condition numbers"
  caption="The sketched estimator remains accurate while a simple LS-inspired estimator can severely overestimate the TLS backward error."
/>

The main message is that the structural normal equation does more than characterize the TLS
solution. It converts backward-error analysis into a modified LS geometry, produces an explicit
optimal formula for stability analysis, and leads to deterministic and randomized estimators that
are practical for large problems. Backward error can therefore serve both as a mathematical tool
for understanding stability and as an inexpensive stopping criterion or accuracy certificate.

## 3. More than just backward error
