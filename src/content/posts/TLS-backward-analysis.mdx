Total least squares (TLS) is often regarded as a natural extension of least squares (LS).
LS has had a profound influence in many fields, thanks to its rich theory and well-developed
algorithms. TLS remains attractive—although harder to solve—because it accounts for errors in all
the data. For a system with a single right-hand side, represented by
$[A,b]$<Sidenote number={1}>My papers address the general multidimensional case $[A,B]$ and allow different weights for the two perturbations.</Sidenote>,
where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $m\gg n$ and $b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$, the two problems can be
written as follows:<Sidenote number={2}>Typically, we write LS as $\min_{x} \|Ax-b\|_{2}$. Here, we use an equivalent formulation to facilitate comparison.</Sidenote>

$$
\min_{\Delta A,\Delta b}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta A & \Delta b
\end{bmatrix}
\right\|_{\mathrm F}
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
(A+\Delta A)x=b+\Delta b,
\tag{TLS}
$$

$$
\min_{\Delta b}
\left\|\Delta b\right\|_2
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
Ax=b+\Delta b.
\tag{LS}
$$
Viewing TLS as a natural extension of LS is compelling at the level of data modeling, and it also
helps explain why statisticians were among the first to study the problem. Yet, as we will see, the
analogy becomes much less convincing at the theoretical and computational levels. Despite a
substantial body of theoretical work, we still lack TLS algorithms that simultaneously satisfy the
practical requirements expected of a satisfactory numerical solver<Sidenote number={3} label="Explanation of a satisfactory numerical solver">By “satisfactory,” I mean methods that are fast for large-scale problems, remain reasonably accurate for at least moderately ill-conditioned problems, and are numerically stable.</Sidenote>.

> A central motivation is: if we could solve a TLS problem as efficiently and reliably as the
> corresponding LS problem on the same data, there would be little reason not to choose TLS.

In my view, the way the subject has developed has partly obscured these computational issues. TLS
deserves more emphasis than it has received—not simply through further abstraction, but through a
reconsideration of which structures are genuinely useful for computation. One such perspective
concerns backward error (see Section 2).

In this post, I will outline some key ideas developed in my recent papers on
[the connections and differences between LS and TLS](/publications/shan2025optimal/)
and
[efficient backward error estimation for multidimensional TLS](/publications/shan2026EfficientBE/),
and offer a few reflections that may be useful for future work.

## 1. From LS to TLS: Are they essentially related, and how?

Through the connection with low-rank approximation—specifically, the
[Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-rank_approximation)—we find that
$[x_{\mathrm{TLS}};-1]$ is parallel to the right singular vector associated with the smallest
singular value of $[A,b]$.
I will not repeat [basic results of TLS](https://doi.org/10.1137/0717073) here.
Consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of this augmented matrix
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A & b
\end{bmatrix}
=
U\Sigma V^\top,
\qquad
V=
\begin{bmatrix}
V_{11} & v_{12}\\
v_{21}^\top & v_{22}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $v_{22}$ is a scalar.
To guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the TLS solution, we assume the _genericity condition_
throughout this post:

$$
\sigma_n\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
A & b
\end{bmatrix}
\right)
>
\sigma_{n+1}\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
A & b
\end{bmatrix}
\right),
\qquad
v_{22}\neq 0.
$$

From this characterization, one obtains the TLS normal equation
$\left(A^\top A-\sigma_{n+1}^{2}([A,b])I\right)x=A^\top b$, which I will call the
_classical normal equation_. For LS, the normal equation is much simpler as $A^\top r=0$, where
$r=b-Ax$ is the residual. To leverage LS theory, we need results that treat TLS as a modified LS
problem. The following theorem does so by providing a _structural normal equation_ and, more
importantly, revealing the intrinsic quantities that govern TLS.

<Statement
  type="theorem"
  number="1.1"
  title="Necessary and sufficient conditions for the TLS solution"
>

Suppose that the genericity condition above holds. Then
$x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the true solution of problem (TLS) if and only if the following two conditions
hold:

$$
\left(A+bx^\top\right)^\top(Ax-b)=0,
\tag{2.1a}
$$

and

$$
\min_{\substack{
  0\neq y\in
  \mathcal{R}\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x;
      -1
    \end{bmatrix}
  \right)
}}
\frac{
  \left\|
    \begin{bmatrix}
      A & b
    \end{bmatrix}y
  \right\|
}{
  \lVert y\rVert
}
<
\min_{\substack{
  0\neq y\in
  \mathcal{R}\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
      I_n;
      x^\top
    \end{bmatrix}
  \right)
}}
\frac{
  \left\|
    \begin{bmatrix}
      A & b
    \end{bmatrix}y
  \right\|
}{
  \lVert y\rVert
}.
\tag{2.1b}
$$
Here, $\mathcal{R}(M)$ denotes the range of $M$.

</Statement>

I shall present only the key parts of the proof that shed light on the structure of TLS. The
extension to the multidimensional case is straightforward.

<Statement type="proof">
Define

$$
T_1
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_n\\
x^\top
\end{bmatrix}
\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}, \quad
T_2
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}\left(I+x^\top x\right)^{-1/2}.
$$

It is straightforward to verify that $T_1^\top T_1=I_n$, $T_2^\top T_2=1$,
$T_1^\top T_2=0$, and $T_1T_1^\top+T_2T_2^\top=I_{n+1}$.

Consequently, $\begin{bmatrix}T_1 & T_2\end{bmatrix}$ is an orthogonal matrix. Now define
$$
\widetilde A=\left(A+bx^\top\right)\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}\coloneqq
\begin{bmatrix}
A & b
\end{bmatrix}T_1,
\quad
\widetilde r=\frac{Ax-b}{\sqrt{1+x^\top x}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}A & b
\end{bmatrix}T_2.
$$

The first condition in the theorem is equivalent to<Sidenote number={4}>It can be reduced further to (2.1a) because the auxiliary factors are invertible.</Sidenote>

$$
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r=0,
$$

whereas the second condition is equivalent to<Sidenote number={5}>It is helpful to view $\|\widetilde r\|_2$ as the largest singular value of $\widetilde r$.</Sidenote>

$$
\left\|\widetilde r\right\|_2
<
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\widetilde A\right).
$$

Therefore, it remains to prove the equivalent characterization

$$
\boxed{
x\text{ is the true TLS solution}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\left\|\widetilde r\right\|_2
<
\sigma_{\min}\left(\widetilde A\right)
}.
$$

</Statement>

One can prove that there always exists a neighborhood of $x_{\mathrm{TLS}}$ in which (2.1b) holds
uniformly. This is good news for iterative methods. One important takeaway is that the _structural
normal equation_ is more naturally expressed not as (2.1a), but as
$$
\widetilde A^\top \widetilde r=0, \quad \mathrm{where} \quad \widetilde{A}=\left(A+bx^\top\right)\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2}, \widetilde{r}=\frac{Ax-b}{\sqrt{1+\|x\|^{2}}},
$$
which shows that TLS involves a nontrivial coupling among $A$, $b$, and the solution $x$, rather
than merely requiring the residual to be orthogonal to a fixed subspace. These differences prevent
the direct application of some well-known techniques that play an important role in making LS
reliable, such as iterative refinement. Nevertheless, it is worth asking under what conditions an
LS solution remains close to the corresponding TLS solution.

<Statement
  type="theorem"
  number="1.2"
  title="Componentwise error between a backward stable LS solution and the true TLS solution"
>

Given a backward stable LS solution $\hat{x}$ to an overdetermined system $[A,b]$, the componentwise
error between $x_{\mathrm{TLS}}$ and $\hat{x}$ along the right singular vectors of $A$ is bounded,
for $i=1,2,\ldots,n$, as follows:

$$
\left|v_i^{\top}\left(\left(1-\frac{{\sigma_{n+1}([A,b])}^{2}}{\sigma_i(A)^{2}}\right)x_{\mathrm{TLS}}-\hat{x}\right)\right|
\lesssim
\frac{1}{\sigma_i}
\|\hat{x}\|\|[A,b]\|\,u+\frac{1}{\sigma_i^{2}}\|b-A\hat{x}\|\|[A,b]\|\,u,
$$
where $u$ is the unit roundoff. The notation $\lesssim$ means that the left-hand side is bounded above by $C(m,n)$ times the right-hand side for some low-degree polynomial $C(m,n)$.
</Statement>

Backward stability will be explained in detail later. Informally, a backward stable method returns
the best kind of solution one can generally expect in finite precision.

The result above shows that information from LS can be exploited under certain conditions. Moreover,
the ordinary condition number, defined as the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value of
a matrix, does not fully characterize the difficulty of a TLS problem. The spectral gap, or the
[effective condition number](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01400115), is more
informative. Specifically, a small LS residual indicates that the LS and TLS solutions are close,
suggesting that a TLS model may be unnecessary for a relatively consistent system.

<Statement
  type="proposition"
  number="1.3"
  title="The residual of the LS solution matters"
>

Let $x_{\mathrm{LS}}$ be the LS solution, and let $r_{\mathrm{LS}}=b-Ax_{\mathrm{LS}}$ be its
residual. Define $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}(A)/\sigma_{\min}(A)$ as the condition number of $A$,
and retain the preceding notation. If $\kappa\|r_{\mathrm{LS}}\|$ is less than and bounded away from
$1$, then the ratio $\sigma_{n+1}([A,b])/\sigma_n(A)$ is also bounded away from $1$.

</Statement>

There are other differences that need to be carefully considered between the solvers of these two problems. For example,
[preconditioning becomes delicate](/publications/shan2025optimal/), but I will not pursue that
topic here.

## 2. Backward error and its efficient computation

Numerical algorithms run in finite precision, so even a mathematically correct method rarely returns
the exact solution corresponding to the data supplied to it. A solver is called
[_backward stable_](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898718027.ch1) if its computed
output is the exact solution of a nearby problem, with the required change in the input comparable
to the rounding errors introduced during the computation. This point of view deliberately separates
the behavior of the algorithm from the conditioning of the problem: a backward stable answer can
still have a large forward error when the underlying problem is ill-conditioned.

Backward stability is one reason to study the backward error of TLS. Before we can prove that a TLS
solver is stable, or decide when an iterative method should stop, we need a quantitative answer to
the following question:

> How much must the original data be perturbed before $\widetilde x$ becomes an exact TLS solution?

A small backward error certifies that the computed answer solves a nearby problem of the
same type, even when comparison with the unknown exact solution is impossible. For stability
analysis, we want an exact characterization; for practical solvers, we also need a reliable estimate
that is inexpensive enough to evaluate repeatedly. In large-scale problems, this second requirement
leads naturally to randomized algorithms.

<Statement type="definition" number="2.1" title="TLS backward error">

For a computed approximation $\widetilde x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, define $\tau(\widetilde x)=\min_{E,f}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}E & f\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm F}$
subject to the structural normal equation for the perturbed data,
$
\left((A+E)+(b+f)\widetilde x^\top\right)^\top
\left((A+E)\widetilde x-(b+f)\right)=0.
$

</Statement>

The normalized quantity $\tau(\widetilde x)/\|[A,b]\|_{\mathrm F}$ measures the size of the smallest
relative change to the data. In the usual normwise sense, a TLS algorithm is backward stable when
this ratio is bounded by a modest dimension-dependent constant times the unit roundoff $u$.

The apparently nonlinear minimization above has a concise closed-form expression. Recall the two quantities
introduced in Section 1, the modified matrix and the scaled residual, allowing for a harmless choice
of sign:
$$
\widetilde A
=
\left(A+b\widetilde x^\top\right)
\left(I_n+\widetilde x\widetilde x^\top\right)^{-1/2},
\quad
\widetilde r
=
\frac{b-A\widetilde x}
{\sqrt{1+\|\widetilde x\|_2^2}}.
$$

<Statement type="theorem" number="2.2" title="Explicit formula for the optimal TLS backward error">

Let $\lambda=\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\widetilde A\widetilde A^\top-\widetilde r\widetilde r^\top\right)$.
Then $\tau(\widetilde x)=\left[\|\widetilde r\|_2^2+\min\{0,\lambda\}\right]^{1/2}$.

</Statement>

I recommend that readers interested in the proof consult the appendix of the original paper.
The formula separates two effects. The first term measures the scaled residual, while the
eigenvalue correction detects whether a coupled perturbation of $A$ and $b$ can make
$\widetilde x$ an exact TLS solution more cheaply than correcting the residual alone. This is why
$\|b-A\widetilde x\|_2$ by itself is not a reliable TLS certificate.

There is nevertheless a computational challenge: evaluating $\lambda$ directly involves an
$m\times m$ eigenvalue problem. For a tall data matrix with $m\gg n$, the exact formula is therefore
impractical to evaluate directly.

<Statement type="theorem" number="2.3" title="A tight deterministic estimate">

For every computed approximation $\widetilde x$, define
$\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)=\left\|\left(\widetilde A^\top\widetilde A+\|\widetilde r\|^2 I\right)^{-1/2}\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r\right\|$.
Then
$$
\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)\leq\tau(\widetilde x)\leq\sqrt{2}\,\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x).
$$

</Statement>

Hence $\widetilde\tau$ is not a heuristic: it approximates the optimal backward error to
within a factor of $\sqrt{2}$. If $\widetilde A=U\Sigma V^\top$, the estimate can also be evaluated
without explicitly forming a matrix
inverse square root:

$$
\widetilde\tau(\widetilde x)^2
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\sigma_i^2}
{\sigma_i^2+\|\widetilde r\|^2}
\left(u_i^\top\widetilde r\right)^2.
$$

This already avoids an $m\times m$ eigendecomposition, but forming a full factorization of
$\widetilde A$ may still be expensive when the number of observations is very large.
Randomized sketching reduces the cost further. Let $S\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times m}$ be a
[subspace embedding](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.01387) for the columns of $[A,b]$ with
distortion $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, and define

$$
\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x)
=
\left\|
\left(
(S\widetilde A)^\top(S\widetilde A)
+
\|\widetilde r\|^2 I
\right)^{-1/2}
\widetilde A^\top\widetilde r
\right\|.
$$

The sketched quantity retains rigorous guarantees:

$$
(1-\varepsilon)\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x)
\leq
\tau(\widetilde x)
\leq
\sqrt{2}(1+\varepsilon)
\widetilde\tau^{\mathrm{sk}}(\widetilde x).
$$

With a sparse sign embedding, the resulting procedure has complexity $\mathcal{O}(mn\log n)$ and
never forms an $m\times m$ matrix.
The factor $\left(I_n+\widetilde x\widetilde x^\top\right)^{-1/2}$ is only a rank-one update of the
identity, so $\widetilde A$ can also be formed efficiently.

> MATLAB implementations of the estimators and the scripts used for the experiments below are available in the
[mTLS Efficient Backward Error Estimate code repository](https://github.com/JudyShan/mTLS-Efficient-Backward-Error-Estimate).

The following experiment uses $m=3000$, $n=20$, and 200 random problems for each of three condition
numbers. The horizontal axis shows the exact backward error divided by its estimate. The blue
distributions correspond to $\tau/\widetilde\tau$, which the theory places between $1$ and
$\sqrt{2}$; the orange distributions use the sketched estimate. In the top row, corresponding to the
vector case studied in this post, the ratios remain tightly concentrated even as $\kappa(A)$
increases from $10^8$ to $10^{16}$.

/>


The runtime grows approximately linearly with $n$ for fixed $m$. The left panel below is the vector
case; the remaining panels show that the same scaling persists for multiple right-hand sides. This
is the practical gain of estimating the backward error through a sketch rather than evaluating the
exact $m\times m$ eigenvalue formula.

Thus, TLS backward error can serve both as a mathematical tool for understanding stability
and as an inexpensive stopping criterion or accuracy certificate within an algorithm.

## 3. More than just backward error

The previous section treats backward error as a number: $\tau(x)$ tells us how far a computed
iterate is from satisfying the defining structure of TLS. The _optimal perturbation_ contains more
information. Its geometry reveals exactly which components of the augmented matrix must be removed,
turning backward analysis from a passive certificate into a possible guide for designing iterative algorithms.

For clarity, we assume throughout this section that condition (2.1b) holds.
Given a current iterate $x$, write
$$
C=\begin{bmatrix}A & b\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
G=\begin{bmatrix}E & f\end{bmatrix},
$$
and reuse the orthogonal factors introduced in Section 1:

$$
T_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_n\\
x^\top
\end{bmatrix}
\left(I_n+xx^\top\right)^{-1/2},
\qquad
T_2=y=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\|x\|_2^2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Here, $T_2=y$ spans the direction determined by the current iterate, while the columns of $T_1$
form an orthonormal basis for $y^\perp$. This decomposition splits every perturbation into two
orthogonal components:

$$
\|G\|_{\mathrm F}^2
=
\left\|G\begin{bmatrix}T_1 & T_2\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm F}^2
=
\|GT_1\|_{\mathrm F}^2+\|GT_2\|_2^2.
$$

At the same time, the structural normal equation becomes
$$
\left((C+G)T_1\right)^\top(C+G)T_2=0.
$$

Thus the backward error problem asks for the smallest change that makes the image of the
one-dimensional direction $y$ orthogonal to the image of its entire orthogonal complement.

<Statement type="proposition" number="3.1" title="Geometry of the optimal perturbation">

Let $G_*$ be an optimal perturbation for the current iterate $x$. Then there is a unit vector
$z\in\mathbb{R}^m$, with $P_z=zz^\top$, such that

$$
G_*T_2=-(I-P_z)CT_2,
\qquad
G_*T_1=-P_zCT_1.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
(I-P_z)(C+G_*)y=0,
\qquad
P_z(C+G_*)(I-yy^\top)=0.
$$

Consequently, $y$ and $z$ are a right and a left singular vector, respectively, of the perturbed
matrix $C+G_*$.

</Statement>

The figure below visualizes the two equivalent viewpoints. Panel (a) shows how a direction $y$ that
is not initially a singular direction of $C$ becomes part of a singular pair for $C+G_*$. Panel (b)
shows the construction more explicitly: $G_*$ removes the component of $Cy$ in $z^\perp$ and the
component of $C(y^\perp)$ along $z$.

<Figure
  path="/assets/img/tls-backward-error-geometry.png"
  alt="Geometric interpretation of the optimal TLS perturbation, showing how two projections are removed to turn the iterate direction into a singular-vector pair"
  caption="Geometry of the optimal TLS perturbation."
/>

This gives a more constructive interpretation of backward error. The vector $z$ and the optimal
perturbation $G_*$ determine one another, while the iterate $x$ determines the normalized direction
$y$. Rather than merely asking whether $x$ has a small backward error, one can ask how to update
$x$, $z$, and the two projection corrections so that the required perturbation shrinks.
This idea can inform the design of shifts in corrected inverse iteration methods when TLS is treated
as a singular value problem.
