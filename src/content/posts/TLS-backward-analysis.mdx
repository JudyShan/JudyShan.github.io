Total least squares (TLS) is often regarded as a natural extension of least squares (LS).
$$
\min_{E,f}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}
E & f
\end{bmatrix}
\right\|_{\mathrm F}
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
(A+E)x=b+f.
\tag{\mathrm{TLS}}
$$

$$
\min_{\Delta b}
\left\|\Delta b\right\|_2
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
Ax=b+\Delta b.
\tag{\mathrm{LS}}
$$
This viewpoint is compelling at the level of data modeling, and it also helps explain why statisticians were among the first to study the problem. 
Yet the analogy could be much less straightforward as we will see.

Despite a substantial body of theoretical work, we still lack TLS algorithms that simultaneously satisfy the practical requirements one would expect of a satisfactory numerical solver.<Sidenote number={1} label="Explanation of a satisfactory numerical solver">By “satisfactory,” I mean methods that are fast in large-scale problems, remain reasonably accurate for at least moderately ill-conditioned problems, and are numerically stable.</Sidenote>
In my view, this gap is partly obscured by the way the subject has developed: increasingly refined theoretical treatments have revealed much of the mathematical subtlety of TLS, but this sophistication has not always translated into comparable progress in practical algorithms. 
TLS therefore deserves more emphasis than it has received—not simply through further abstraction, but through a reconsideration of which structures are actually useful for computation.

One such viewpoint concerns backward error. 
I originally approached backward error as a first step toward a more complete stability theory for TLS. During this work, however, I came to see that it may play a more constructive role. 
Backward error need not be only a diagnostic tool used after an algorithm has been designed; it can also reveal exploitable structure, suggest computable quantities that reflect proximity to the TLS solution, and potentially guide the design of the iteration itself. 
Developing this perspective — and exploring how it can lead to new approaches for TLS and related problems in scientific computing — is one of the main themes of my ongoing work.

In this post, I will begin by laying out the ideas developed in my recent papers and leave some reflections that could be of great help.
